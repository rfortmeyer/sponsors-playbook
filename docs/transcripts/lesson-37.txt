The Sponsor's Playbook
Lesson 37: Dividend recapitalizations

Welcome to The Sponsor's Playbook. This is lesson 37: Dividend recapitalizations.

Picture this. It's a Tuesday in October, and your CEO pulls you into a short call with the sponsor. The company has had two strong years. Branch startups are maturing, the new technology platform is live, and EBITDA is up about forty percent since the deal closed. The sponsor says, "Credit markets are open. Lenders are calling us. What would it take to pull some money out?"

Nobody is selling the company. Nobody is buying anything. They want to borrow more money and send it to the shareholders. That is a dividend recapitalization, and as COO and General Counsel, you are about to be asked to make it work.

Let's start with the definition. A dividend recapitalization, or dividend recap, is when a company takes on new debt and uses the proceeds to pay a dividend to its equity holders. The business doesn't change. The assets don't change. What changes is the mix of debt and equity: more debt, less equity, and cash in the owners' hands.

Why would a sponsor do it? Because of timing. Private equity returns are judged on two things: how much money comes back, and how fast. That second measure is internal rate of return, or IRR. If a sponsor gets a fifth of its investment back in year three instead of waiting for an exit in year five, the IRR goes up, even if the total dollars at the end are about the same. It also reduces risk. Money already returned can't be lost in a later downturn. And it can help when the exit market is poor and the sponsor doesn't want to sell at a bad price.

Mechanically, there are a few ways to do it. The most common is to borrow more under the existing credit agreement. Many agreements include an incremental facility, sometimes called an accordion, that lets the company add term loans if it meets conditions, usually a leverage test. Another way is a full refinancing: new, larger loans repay the old ones, and the extra cash goes out the door. We'll cover refinancing next lesson. A third way is debt raised above the operating company, at a holding company, sometimes as a PIK note, where the interest is added to the balance instead of paid in cash. That keeps the operating company's lenders out of the picture, but it adds risk, and the operating company's credit agreement still limits how much cash can move up to the holding company.

And remember lesson 36. Preferred equity can be another way to fund a distribution. It sits further down the capital stack, but the PIK math we talked about applies there too.

Now the part that matters most to you. A dividend is a restricted payment under the credit agreement. Lesson 34 told you to read negative covenants as a permission structure, and this is the biggest permission test of all. Lenders generally don't want cash leaving the company. So agreements limit dividends through baskets. There may be a small general basket. There may be a builder basket, which grows with retained earnings or a share of excess cash flow. And there may be an unlimited ratio-based basket, which allows dividends if pro forma leverage is below a stated number and no default exists. Market practice varies a lot by deal size, by sector, and by where we are in the credit cycle. In a hot market, sponsors negotiate loose restricted payment terms. In a tight market, lenders hold firm. So the first question is never "do we want to?" It's "what does the document allow, and who has to consent if it doesn't?"

Let's walk through an example with round numbers.

Your company, a freight brokerage, has twenty-nine million dollars of EBITDA as defined in the credit agreement. Let's say thirty, to keep it clean. Existing debt is one hundred twenty million dollars. That's four times leverage. The all-in interest rate on that debt is ten percent, so interest is twelve million a year. The maintenance covenant allows leverage up to six times.

The sponsor wants to add thirty million dollars of new term loan. After about one million in fees and expenses, that leaves twenty-nine million to distribute. Debt goes from one hundred twenty to one hundred fifty million. Leverage goes from four times to five times. That passes the six times covenant, and let's say it also fits the incremental test at five and a quarter times. So on day one, it works.

Now look at the cash. The new thirty million at ten percent adds three million dollars of annual interest. Interest goes from twelve to fifteen million. Even after the tax benefit of deducting that interest, which has its own limits, free cash flow drops by roughly two and a half million. If the business was producing about ten million of free cash flow after interest, capex, capitalized software, taxes, and required principal, it now produces about seven and a half. That's about a quarter less cash. Remember, your revolver is for working capital. If free cash flow is thinner, a slow-paying customer or a rate squeeze will push you toward the revolver faster.

Now run the downside, the way lessons 32 and 33 taught you. Freight is cyclical. Brokerage margins can compress fast when spot rates rise and contract rates lag. Say EBITDA falls twenty percent, from thirty million to twenty-four. Without the recap, leverage is one hundred twenty over twenty-four, which is five times. That passes the six times covenant with room to spare. With the recap, it's one hundred fifty over twenty-four, which is six point two five times. That breaches. Same business, same downturn, same management. The only difference is the dividend. Without it, you're managing a rough year. With it, you're calling lenders about a default, or negotiating an equity cure, where the sponsor puts money back in. Now, the sponsor took twenty-nine million out and might have to put some back. That's why the downside case is the real test of a dividend recap, not the day-one math.

Look at it from the sponsor's chair. If the sponsor put in one hundred fifty million of equity, twenty-nine million back is about a fifth of the investment returned. That's meaningful, and it's why the idea is so tempting. But the sponsor is also thinking about whether the business can carry it, whether lenders will push back, and whether the company would still have room to do add-ons or invest in branches. Every dollar of leverage used for a dividend is a dollar of capacity not available for growth. Back in lessons 29 and 32, we saw an add-on fail an incurrence test at four point five two times against a four and a half times condition. If you recap to five times and then find the perfect acquisition, you may have used up the capacity that would have funded it.

Now, what does this mean for you, as the COO and General Counsel?

First, you will be asked to supply the numbers lenders will test. In a recap, lenders look hard at EBITDA, because they are being asked to lend more against it. Expect a fresh quality of earnings review. Addbacks for branch startup losses, for synergies, for one-time costs, all get scrutinized. If your startup branch addback has a cap, as in lesson 34, make sure the number you present fits it. Lenders who feel an EBITDA number was stretched to size a dividend will remember it for every future amendment.

Second, the legal part is yours. A dividend isn't just a credit agreement question. State corporate law limits dividends. In many states, including Delaware, a company can pay dividends only out of surplus, and directors can be personally exposed if they approve an unlawful one. Beyond that, fraudulent transfer law can apply if the company is left insolvent or with unreasonably small capital, and a later bankruptcy trustee can try to claw the money back. So boards often get a solvency analysis, sometimes a formal solvency opinion from a third party, and documented board deliberation. You should make sure the board has real projections, including a downside, and that the minutes show it considered them. The board's job here is to look at the company's health, not just the sponsor's return. That matters even more when there are minority holders or management shareholders.

Third, management equity. This is where it gets personal for your team. When cash leaves the company, the value of the remaining equity drops. Option holders didn't receive the dividend. Many plans handle this by adjusting strike prices, paying dividend equivalents on vested or unvested awards, or doing a combination. Some plans require an adjustment, some allow it, and some are silent. Tax rules matter here, including section 409A for options, so involve your tax advisors. If your producers and leaders hold equity, and they see the sponsor taking money out while theirs gets diluted, you have a retention problem. Know what the plan says, and know what the sponsor intends, before the announcement.

Fourth, think about how each party sees it. The sponsor sees returns and timing. The board sees fiduciary duty, risk, and whether the company is left healthy. Lenders see leakage. They want the company's cash to stay inside the company, so they push for conditions: pro forma leverage below a level, no default, minimum liquidity after the dividend, maybe a higher interest margin or a call premium on the new loan. A good lender question is, "If EBITDA falls twenty percent, what's liquidity?" Have the thirteen-week cash flow and a revolver availability analysis ready, per lesson 35.

Fifth, in freight, lenders will ask about cyclicality, customer concentration, and carrier claims and insurance exposure. Be ready to explain how net revenue holds up when the market turns. That's a story you know better than anyone.

Now, common mistakes.

The first is sizing the recap to the covenant maximum. If the agreement allows five and a quarter times and the sponsor wants five and a quarter, you've left no cushion. A small miss triggers a problem. Size it to the downside case, not to what the document permits.

The second is treating it as separate from the operating plan. A recap affects interest, cash, covenants, and flexibility. If you have branch openings, a technology build, or acquisitions planned, put them in the same model. Otherwise you find out later that the dividend consumed the room you needed.

The third is forgetting the people who didn't get paid. Management equity, option adjustments, and employee communications are often an afterthought. They shouldn't be. A recap that costs you your top producers' loyalty can cost more than it returned.

A fourth, briefly: assuming the lenders will be flexible next time. If the downside comes, the same lenders you brought in will be negotiating from a stronger position, and a recap-sized balance sheet gives you less to negotiate with.

So, to recap. A dividend recap is new debt used to pay equity holders. It boosts sponsor returns and speeds up cash back, but it raises leverage, interest, and risk, and it uses up capacity you might need. The credit agreement decides whether you can do it, through restricted payment baskets and incremental debt tests. Corporate law and solvency decide whether you should. And the downside case decides whether it's wise. In our example, a thirty million dollar recap took leverage from four times to five, cut free cash flow by about a quarter, and turned a passing five times in a twenty percent downturn into a six point two five times breach. As COO and General Counsel, you supply the earnings numbers, run the legal process, protect management equity, and make sure the board and lenders see a real downside case.

Here's your question for today. If the sponsor called tomorrow and asked about a recap, what is the one number you would want to see first, and what would that number have to be for you to say, "We can do this and still operate the business we planned"?

Key takeaways:
- A dividend recap uses new debt to pay equity holders, which speeds up sponsor returns but raises leverage and interest and uses up capacity for add-ons and growth.
- Permission comes from the credit agreement's restricted payment baskets and incremental debt tests, but whether it is wise depends on state dividend law, solvency, and the downside case. In our example, leverage of five times became a six point two five times breach after a twenty percent EBITDA decline.
- As COO and General Counsel, supply defensible EBITDA and cash forecasts, document the board's solvency review, handle management equity and option adjustments, and size the dividend to the downside, not to the covenant limit.
